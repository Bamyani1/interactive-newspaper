Bad precedent

The recent forced resignations of three Tau Kappa Epsilon (TKE) officers by Dennis Perry, TKE district representative, is a dangerous precedent for the Greek system at OWU.

Perry is working with the OWU TKE chapter, which is on probation by its national headquarters.

According to Gary Rybak, coordinator of Greek affairs, the Greek system is designed to teach students "organizational growth, personal responsibility and application of the democratic process."

How can students learn about personal responsibility when nearly all of theirs is taken away?

How can students learn about organizational growth when they have no control of their own organization's growth?

How can students learn about the democratic process when one man strips their elected leaders of their rank and implements what amounts to totalitarianism?

Perry has good intentions. He wants to help TKE. However, his statement to the Transcript, "I will have absolute authority," paired with the officers' forced resignations, reeks of Machiavelianism.

Because TKE has been on probation, there is surely a problem. It can best be solved through guidance and education, not by the establishment of martial law.

"Rock and Roll OWU?"

I do not, as a general rule, claim to be a movie critic, but every now and then a film comes along which, intentionally or not, makes an important point about life. For me, "Rock and Roll High School" is such a film.

In case you don't know, the movie is about a high school, located somewhere in the U.S., where the entire student body is fanatically devoted to the Ramones (if you don't know who the Ramones are, you probably don't listen to WSLN enough). The school's principal, unfortunately, is a stuffy, 30ish, neo-Nazi woman who wants to eradicate rock music and thereby save her youthful charges from destroying their minds.

Using every available high school movie cliche, "Rock and Roll High School" presents a modern version of the morality play, wherein evil (personified by the principal, the school board and two traitorous hall monitors) is overcome by good (the kids, a hip teacher and, of course, the Ramones).

"Rock and Roll High School" impressed me, not so much because it was a good time (which it was) nor because of any artistic value (it has none), but because it reminded me of dear old OWU.

That's because dear old OWU is presently on a campaign to keep you and me from destroying our minds. This academic year alone, we have seen new policies put forth on how fraternities should behave and on the consumption of alcohol, among others. These measures, however well-intentioned, basically leave you about three choices: get bombed (quietly), watch the tube or wait for the next rolling blackout to hit.

Not that there isn't a lot of potential entertainment around here: OWU has a fairly decent radio station, a concert hall famous for its acoustics and committees to bring in movies, concerts, lecturers, artists, etc.

Yet something is wrong. The lectures, movies and concerts that are brought here are plagued by poor attendance, having to rely, it has been said, on one or two well-attended big-name events per year for support.

The solution I have in mind has two parts, neither of which will be easily accomplished.

First, OWU needs a new, more comprehensive way of bringing talent here. The present multiplicity of committees may have seemed like a good idea once, but if nobody wants to see the acts that they bring here, what's the use?

Second, we (meaning those of you who will be around here past June) may have to be ready to cough up more cash for entertainment than in the past; you get what you pay for.

Unfortunately, most of what the University has done recently to improve our lives here has been punitive: don't make noise, don't drink in public, don't get caught with more than six people in your room (and then they wonder why the residential part of campus goes crazy every weekend), etc.

You see, that's the mistake the principal made in "Rock and Roll High School:" when she tried to impose a "final solution" to her problems by burning the kids' records, they responded by burning the school.

Get the picture?

Pretenders successfully combine old and New

New Wave music is an enigma to many album buyers, and to give this general term a definition would be nearly impossible. What the music industry is experiencing is a resurrection of early to mid-Sixties rock and roll, circa the first British Invasion led by The Rolling Stones and The Kinks. True, much of today's New Wave sound owes its roots to Seventies stars like David Bowie, Roxy Music and Mott The Hoople, but a return to the basics is New Wave's most dominant premise.

The Pretenders are the first New Wave band to fuse together sounds from both the Sixties and Seventies while offering new ground for the future. The album, "The Pretenders" and its initial single, "Stop Your Sobbing" (written by The Kinks' Ray Davies), entered the English charts at number one simultaneously. The Pretenders now find themselves in the top-30 position in the United States, and consequently, they have received heavy critical response and considerable airplay.

The Pretenders' success is due mainly to lead singer Chrissie Hynde, whose voice recalls early Grace Slick and Debbie Harry so convincingly that when she sings, "a good time is guaranteed for one and all" in "Tattooed Love Boys," you better believe you're going to have a good time.

The Pretenders are not a one-woman show, however. The remaining three-fourths are built around James Honeyman Scott's smart, concise guitar runs; Pete Farndon's throbbing bass and Martin Chambers' rolling drums. The instrumental track, "Space Invader," showcases the band's musical talent, sans the excess found in so many debut albums.

Scott's introduction to "Precious" gets the album off to a blistering start, and he continues right into "Up The Neck," probably the most cohesive song on the album.

The current single, "Brass in Pocket," contains a memorable beat which Hynde enhances with her superb crooning.

The enormous hit, "Stop Your Sobbing," is the only song without production by Chris Thomas (Nick Lowe has the honors on this one). Thomas, noted for his work with the Sex Pistols, gives "The Pretenders" needed expertise, without overproduction. "Stop Your Sobbing" takes me back to the early English rock and roll played on the Ed Sullivan Show, with Phil Spector overtones filtering throughout.

Although the second album for any group is always the test for greatness, I can't see The Pretenders getting caught in the success trap which would inhibit them from producing quality New Wave music. Not since "The Cars" has a debut album made such an impression on me. Repeated listening is the prescription for this debut from The Pretenders: rock's alternative to excessive indulgence from so many mediocre, money-making garage bands.
