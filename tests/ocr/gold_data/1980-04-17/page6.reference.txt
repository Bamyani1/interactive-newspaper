Nader urges students to use rights as citizens

Even as she pushed a fallen lock of hair away from her face, obviously fatigued, anthropologist Laura Nader was dignified. Never tiring of talking about her cause, that is, arousing the inquiring minds within young people, Nader and I talked about nuclear energy, politics, women's rights, religion and children. No superficial interview, this.

Nader spoke at 8:15 p.m. last Thursday in the Memorial Union Building (MUB) ballroom on "Energy and the Quality of Life," sponsored by Phi Beta Kappa.

"First, I am an educator," she said. "In the classroom, I find it is more important than anything to teach people to question authority and weigh facts."

The importance of having an inquiring mind is central to Nader's philosophy. "As an anthropologist, I teach the range and diversity among societies," she said. "I try to teach what a precious thing diversity is and, through that knowledge, bring about an understanding of what humans are capable of doing."

"As a woman," she said, "I strive to point out what we, as a society, lose when 50 percent of our population is born handicapped by virtue of being female."

"Finally, as a citizen, my call is to communicate to other citizens the sociopolitical implications of what is happening in our world. I feel pressed to contribute something in the way of energy - nuclear energy in particular."

Nader spent four years at a California conference on energy and recalls, "It was the most difficult work I have ever done. The scientists and engineers work with numbers - they treat people like objects. At one point, a colleague in the 'risk' division pointed out if we could prevent a certain number of deaths in other areas, we could 'afford' a nuclear disaster. Nobody there said a thing!

"No one asks why we don't consider conservation as a source of supply, or why we haven't done anything about solar energy," she said, returning to her main theme of the importance of inquiry. "The main question we must ask is, 'Who decides?' Who decides what kind of energy we will use, or whether women are discriminated against, or whether our democracy is in danger?"

On that note, Nader hastened to assert that U.S. democracy is, indeed, in danger.

"You can't have a democracy without an active citizen body," she said. "The old adage is true: 'If we don't use our rights, we lose our rights.' For 30 years, nuclear energy has gone on without citizen input. We are reaping the harvest of years of apathy."

Nevertheless, Nader expressed optimism about the possibilities of improvement if we do become more inquisitive. "You have to be optimistic," she said. "If you only talk about the future in bleak terms, it will be bleak. It's a self-fulfilling prophesy."

"People, especially students, think they can't do anything. Look at the students of the Sixties and early Seventies. They brought an end to Viet Nam. It was the students who did that."

Leaning forward once again, she clasped the arms of her chair as if to urge all the more. "If we could only cling to that child-like curiosity," she said, her voice full of hope. "My littlest girl looked out our window one day and asked, 'Mommy, why do we have pollution?' Well, I began to explain that cars put too much pollution in the air and they could be made cleaner but they aren't.... Before I knew it I had explained our whole society."
